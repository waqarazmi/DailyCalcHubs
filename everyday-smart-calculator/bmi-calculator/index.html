How the Body Mass Index (BMI) Works

Body Mass Index (BMI) is an anthropometric screening indicator developed to gauge whether a person has a healthy body weight for their height. First formulated by Belgian mathematician Adolphe Quetelet, it is universally adopted by the World Health Organization (WHO), national health authorities, and clinical screening bodies as an initial measure of body composition.

Mathematically Valid BMI Formulas

Metric: BMI = Weight (kg) ÷ [Height (m)]²
Imperial: BMI = [Weight (lbs) ÷ Height (in)²] × 703

Step-by-Step Worked Example

Consider an adult with a height of 175 cm (1.75 meters) and a body weight of 70 kg:

  • Step 1 (Convert height to meters): 175 cm = 1.75 m.
  • Step 2 (Square the height): 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625 m².
  • Step 3 (Divide weight by height squared): 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.86 kg/m².
  • Classification: 22.86 falls comfortably within the WHO 18.5 to 24.9 range, indicating a Normal / Healthy Weight.

World Health Organization (WHO) BMI Classification

Classification BMI Range (kg/m²) Health Risk Context
Underweight < 18.5 Increased risk of nutritional deficiency and osteoporosis
Normal Weight 18.5 – 24.9 Lowest statistical risk of cardiovascular illness
Overweight 25.0 – 29.9 Moderate risk; lifestyle and dietary intervention beneficial
Obese Class I 30.0 – 34.9 Elevated cardiovascular, blood pressure, and diabetes risk
Obese Class II 35.0 – 39.9 High risk; medical guidance strongly recommended
Obese Class III (Severe) ≥ 40.0 Critical clinical risk requiring supervised medical care

Important Limitations of BMI

While BMI provides an effective population-level screening benchmark, it possesses natural clinical limitations that every user should understand:

  • Muscle vs. Fat Composition: Muscle tissue is substantially denser than adipose tissue. Athletes, bodybuilders, and active manual workers frequently record BMIs above 25 or 30 despite maintaining exceptionally lean body fat levels.
  • Age and Bone Density: Older adults naturally lose lean muscle mass (sarcopenia). A senior citizen with an apparently "normal" BMI may possess higher abdominal fat percentage than an active younger adult.
  • Fat Distribution: Visceral fat around abdominal organs carries greater cardiovascular risk than subcutaneous fat on the hips or limbs. Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) provides valuable complementary insight.

Educational Health Disclaimer

Use BMI as a general educational screening measure, not as a medical diagnosis. Body Mass Index does not measure individual body fat percentage, metabolic health, or clinical fitness. Individuals should never initiate extreme diets or medical interventions based solely on a calculator score. Always consult a certified physician, licensed dietitian, or registered healthcare provider for clinical assessments.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Body Mass Index (BMI)?
Body Mass Index (BMI) is an internationally recognized screening metric defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) that compares an individual's weight relative to their height in kilograms per square meter (kg/m²).
What is considered a healthy BMI range?
According to the World Health Organization standard for adults aged 20 and older, a normal, healthy BMI is between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m².
Does BMI differentiate between muscle and body fat?
No. BMI does not measure body fat percentage directly. Athletes and individuals with high muscularity often test as overweight or obese on BMI scales despite carrying low body fat.
How is my healthy target weight range calculated?
The target range is computed by applying the WHO healthy boundaries (18.5 and 24.9) to your specific height squared: Minimum Weight = 18.5 × Height(m)²; Maximum Weight = 24.9 × Height(m)².
